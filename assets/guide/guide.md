# Yosemite Survival Guide

Each section starts with `## <id> | <title>`. The first paragraph is the short spoken summary.

## lost_stop | Lost: S.T.O.P.
Stop where you are, sit down and breathe. Think about how you got here, observe your surroundings, then plan. Staying put makes you much easier to find.

- **S**top: do not keep walking; most lost hikers walk further from searchers.
- **T**hink: when and where did you last know your position? Which trail, which junction?
- **O**bserve: landmarks (Half Dome, El Capitan, rivers, roads you can hear), the sun's direction, time until sunset.
- **P**lan: if you know a trail or road is close and the route is clear, go to it. Otherwise stay, make yourself visible and wait.
- Water runs downhill to rivers, and in Yosemite most valleys lead to roads, but canyons and cliffs are dangerous; do not follow drainages down steep terrain.
- Tell someone: if you have any signal, call 911. A Pixel can use Satellite SOS with no cell signal: open the phone app, dial 911 and follow the satellite prompts with a clear view of the sky.

## water_purify | Finding and treating water
Drink from flowing streams or lakes and treat it if you can. If you cannot treat it, dehydration is the bigger danger: drink anyway, taken from fast-moving water upstream of trails and camps.

- Best sources: springs, fast-flowing streams, the upstream end of lakes. Avoid stagnant water, water near meadows with cattle or below campsites.
- Treat by boiling (one minute rolling boil; three minutes above 2000 m), a filter, or chlorine dioxide tablets (wait 30 minutes; 4 hours for cold or cloudy water).
- Giardia takes 1 to 2 weeks to cause symptoms; dehydration can disable you within a day.
- Signs of dehydration: dark urine, headache, dizziness, confusion. Drink small amounts often.
- Do not eat snow without melting it; it chills your core. Melt it in a container in the sun or near your body.
- In late summer many small streams dry up; the Merced and Tuolumne rivers and large lakes run all year.

## hypothermia | Cold and hypothermia
Get out of wind and wet, put on every dry layer, insulate yourself from the ground, and eat and drink something warm or sugary if you have it. Shivering is a warning sign; stopping shivering while still cold is an emergency.

- Yosemite nights can drop below freezing even in summer above 2500 m.
- Wet clothes lose most of their insulation: change into dry layers, or wring out wet ones.
- Sit on a pack, branches or pine needles: the ground drains heat fast.
- Huddle with others; put warm water bottles at armpits and groin.
- Severe hypothermia (confusion, slurred speech, no shivering): handle gently, keep horizontal, warm the core, call for help.
- Frostbite: rewarm only if it will not refreeze; never rub the skin.

## heat | Heat exhaustion and heat stroke
Stop, get into shade, cool down with water on your skin and clothes, and sip water. If the person is confused or has stopped sweating, it is heat stroke, a life-threatening emergency: cool them aggressively and call for help.

- Valley floor and lower canyons (Hetch Hetchy, Merced canyon) exceed 35 °C in summer.
- Hike in the early morning and evening; rest in shade from late morning to mid afternoon.
- Heat exhaustion: heavy sweating, weakness, nausea, headache. Rest, cool, drink, eat salty food.
- Heat stroke: hot skin, confusion, collapse. Immerse in cool water if possible, fan, call 911.

## bear | Black bear encounter
Stay calm, do not run. Make yourself look big, wave your arms and shout firmly. Give the bear an escape route and back away slowly. Yosemite has black bears only; they almost always flee.

- If a bear approaches your food, make noise: yell, clap, bang pots. Groups should stand together.
- Never get between a mother and cubs.
- If a black bear attacks (very rare), fight back with everything you have; do not play dead.
- Store all food and scented items in a bear canister or locker; never in a tent.

## mountain_lion | Mountain lion encounter
Do not run and do not crouch. Face it, make yourself look large, pick up small children, speak loudly and firmly, and back away slowly. If it attacks, fight back.

- Throw stones or branches without bending down far.
- Keep eye contact; never turn your back.
- Attacks are extremely rare; most lions avoid people.

## injury | Sprains, fractures and injuries
Stop and assess: check breathing, bleeding and the injury. Keep the injured limb still, splint it with sticks and clothing, and do not try to hike out on a badly injured leg if you can signal for help instead.

- Sprain: rest, compress with a wrap, elevate; walk only if you can bear weight.
- Suspected fracture: splint the joint above and below the break in the position found; check fingers or toes stay warm and pink.
- Head injury: watch for confusion, vomiting, uneven pupils. Keep them awake and still; call for help.
- Suspected spine injury: do not move them unless in immediate danger.
- Keep the injured person warm and insulated from the ground; shock causes cold.

## bleeding | Severe bleeding
Press hard directly on the wound with cloth and do not let go for at least ten minutes. If bleeding from an arm or leg will not stop, tie a tight band five centimeters above the wound and twist it with a stick until the bleeding stops; note the time.

- Pack deep wounds with cloth and keep pressing.
- Do not remove soaked dressings; add more on top.
- Lay the person down and keep them warm.
- Call 911 or use Satellite SOS immediately for severe bleeding.

## signaling | Signaling for rescue
Make yourself big and visible: stay in the open, spread bright clothing on the ground, and use three of anything as a distress signal: three whistle blasts, three fires, three shouts, repeated. Flash a mirror or phone screen at aircraft.

- Whistle carries far further than your voice and uses less energy.
- Signal mirror: aim the reflection through a V made by your fingers at the aircraft or searchers.
- Ground signal: a large X or SOS made from rocks, logs or clothing in a clearing.
- At night, a flashlight or phone light flashed three times.
- Smoke by day (green branches on a fire), flame by night; only where fire is safe and legal.
- Keep your phone on airplane mode between checks to save battery; turn it on periodically on high ground to look for signal, and try Satellite SOS with a clear view of the sky.

## shelter | Emergency shelter
Get out of wind and rain before dark: use a rock overhang, a dense tree's base or a fallen log, and pile branches and needles for a roof and thick insulation underneath you.

- Choose a spot away from dead trees (widowmakers), dry stream beds and avalanche or rockfall paths.
- Small shelters stay warmer. The ground insulation matters more than the roof.
- Do not shelter in a cave that floods or under lone trees in a thunderstorm.

## night | Spending the night
If less than an hour of daylight remains and you are not sure of the route, stop and prepare to spend the night: find shelter, gather insulation, put on all your layers, and drink. Walking in the dark is how most falls happen.

- Gather materials while it is still light.
- Nights are long; ration headlamp and phone battery.
- Expect cold: even warm days can have near-freezing nights at altitude.
- In the morning, move to an open spot where searchers can see you.

## river_crossing | River and stream crossings
Avoid crossing fast or deep water. Rivers like the Merced are cold and powerful, and drown more people in Yosemite than any other hazard. Look for a bridge, or cross early in the morning when flows are lowest.

- Never cross above a waterfall or rapids.
- If you must cross: unbuckle your pack's hip belt, face upstream, use a stick as a third leg, and move one point at a time.
- Water above the knees is dangerous; above the thighs, do not cross.
- If swept away: float on your back, feet downstream, and steer to shore.
- Stay off wet, polished rocks near waterfalls (Vernal and Nevada Falls).

## lightning | Thunderstorms and lightning
Get off peaks, ridges and domes immediately. Descend, avoid lone trees and water, and if caught in the open, crouch on your pack with feet together. Count seconds from flash to thunder: under 30 seconds, you are in danger.

- Afternoon storms are common in summer in the high country.
- Half Dome cables and summit: never ascend if storms threaten; wet granite is extremely slippery.
- Spread a group out at least 15 meters apart.
- Wait 30 minutes after the last thunder before moving back to exposed ground.

## fire | Making a fire
Only make a fire if you need warmth or a signal and it is safe: clear a circle down to bare dirt, away from overhanging branches, and keep it small. Start with tiny dry twigs, then pencil-thick sticks, then wrist-thick wood.

- Fire danger is extreme in late summer; fires above 9600 ft are banned in Yosemite.
- Dry tinder: dead pine needles, inner bark, pitch from pine stumps.
- Never leave a fire; drown it with water and stir until cold when you leave.
- A wildfire nearby: move away from it, downhill and into burned or rocky areas, not up narrow canyons.

## altitude | Altitude sickness
If you have a headache, nausea, or dizziness above 2500 meters, stop climbing and rest. If symptoms get worse or include confusion, stumbling or breathlessness at rest, descend at once.

- Tioga Road and Tuolumne Meadows are above 2600 m.
- Drink water and eat; avoid going higher until symptoms clear.
- Descending even 300 to 500 meters usually brings rapid relief.
